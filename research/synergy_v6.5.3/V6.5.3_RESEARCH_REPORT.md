# Synergy V6.5.3 — Credit Risk & Liquidity Engine

## Executive result
Synthetic Monte Carlo research prototype. It is not a calibrated forecast or a real-company performance claim.

- Main grid: **8,000 paired scenarios**, 3 policies per scenario = **24,000 policy paths**.
- Concentration tail study: **4,000 paired scenarios**, 3 policies per scenario = **12,000 policy paths**.
- Total before systemic stress: **12,000 unique paired scenarios and 36,000 policy paths**. The additional systemic-default experiment adds **3,000 scenarios and 9,000 policy paths**, bringing the full V6.5.3 research pass to **15,000 unique paired scenarios and 45,000 policy paths**.
- Main grid uses 25 common random seeds per parameter cell. Random-number seeds are held constant across PD, recovery, and fee/discount levels, so parameter sweeps use common random numbers.
- Tail study uses 1,000 seeds for each of four customer concentration structures.

## Three policies compared
1. **Baseline:** customer invoices settle in 14 days.
2. **Early pay:** customer pays in 7 days in exchange for a tested invoice discount.
3. **Non-recourse factoring:** factor advances 80% at invoice issue, deducts its fee, and releases the 20% reserve on day 14 if the customer pays. If the customer defaults before maturity, the merchant loses the reserve while the factor bears the advanced 80% loss.

All three policies share the same customer defaults, customer-specific sales path, supplier payment lag, and operating costs within each paired scenario.

## Main grid averages
Across all PD, recovery, concentration and tested fee/discount levels:
- Factoring reduces peak modeled external liquidity need by **708.51** (95% CI **707.65–709.37**).
- Early-pay reduces it by **707.62** (95% CI **707.20–708.05**).
- Average profit change is **-99.59** for factoring (95% CI **-103.53–-95.65**) and **-105.48** for early-pay (95% CI **-108.87–-102.08**).

These averages weight each tested parameter combination equally; they are not forecasts of a likely portfolio. The key finding is that liquidity relief can be large while the transaction's economic value is negative once fees, discounts, and retained credit losses are counted.

## Fee/discount sensitivity
At PD 5% over the 120-day horizon and recovery 30%, the interpolated break-even fee/discount is approximately:
- Factoring: **0.35%** of invoice face.
- Early-pay discount: **0.25%**.

At PD 10%, recovery 30%, the thresholds rise to:
- Factoring: **0.63%**.
- Early-pay: **0.44%**.

These are model-specific interpolations across a sparse fee grid. They are not market quotes or recommended contract prices.

## Model assumptions
- 120 operating days plus a 30-day settlement/recovery tail.
- Nominal revenue capacity: 150 currency units per operating day before customer defaults.
- Variable supplier costs: 60% of realized sales, paid after 7 days.
- Fixed costs: 20 per operating day; opening cash: 200.
- Baseline terms: 14 days; early-pay terms: 7 days.
- PD is a per-customer probability of default during the 120-day horizon, not annualized PD.
- Recovery is a fraction of defaulted invoice face value received 30 days after default.
- Customer-level default stops future purchases from that customer. Lost future demand is not reallocated to other customers.
- Concentration structures: one customer (100%); 10 customers with largest at 50%; 50 customers with largest at 25%; 100 customers with largest at 10%.

## Limitations
- No taxes, payroll, interest on liquidity gap, collateral, covenants, collection costs, fraud, or common macroeconomic default factor.
- No buyer price elasticity or behavioral response to early-payment discounts.
- No production/inventory capacity feedback, supplier insolvency, or inter-company contagion.
- The liquidity gap is a simulated funding need, not a bank underwriting metric.
- The factor is assumed genuinely non-recourse for the advanced portion; legal enforceability and dilution/repurchase clauses are not modeled.
- Currency units are abstract; all parameters are synthetic.

## Systemic default-correlation stress
A further **3,000 scenarios** were run: 500 seeds at each latent common-factor loading
rho = 0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.0. The portfolio has 100 customers, with the largest
customer representing 10% of sales. Per-customer 120-day PD is 5%, recovery is 30%,
and factoring fee is 0.5%.

The rho parameter is the loading/correlation of latent asset variables in a one-factor
Gaussian copula. It is **not** the observed pairwise default correlation. At rho=1,
defaults are driven entirely by the common factor; the model produces near all-or-none
systemic default clusters.

| Latent factor rho | Mean bad debt | 95% CVaR bad debt | 95% CVaR lost sales |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.0 | 69.96 | 209.82 | 1409.90 |\n| 0.1 | 69.82 | 269.40 | 1849.88 |\n| 0.3 | 66.32 | 403.59 | 2832.64 |\n| 0.6 | 80.75 | 771.82 | 5070.63 |\n| 0.9 | 78.02 | 1014.94 | 6767.88 |\n| 1.0 | 82.84 | 1392.06 | 9180.84 |\n
At rho=0, 95% CVaR of bad debt is **209.82**.
At rho=1, it is **1392.06**, a multiple of
**6.63x**.
The corresponding 95% CVaR of lost sales rises from
**1409.90** to
**9180.84**.

This demonstrates why independent-customer PD assumptions are insufficient for a
shared insurance or credit pool: expected loss can move modestly while tail loss expands
dramatically. A real model needs sector/geography/supplier common factors, wrong-way risk,
and funded capital sized to tail exposure.

## Systemic stress limitations
- Gaussian one-factor dependence is a simplified dependence model, not an empirical default copula.
- The 500 seeds per rho provide exploratory tail estimates, not stable extreme-value calibration.
- No contagion through suppliers, banks, household income, or collateral prices is modeled.
- rho=1 is a deliberately extreme common-factor boundary case.
